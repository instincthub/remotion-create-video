# AI Playbook - Brand Voice Document

> This document defines how the AI Playbook communicates across every channel, every format, and every audience. It is the single source of truth for tone, vocabulary, writing style, and what to avoid. Anyone writing content for the AI Playbook — including Claude — must apply this document before producing any output.
> 

---

## 1. Brand identity in one sentence

The AI Playbook is the most practical, honest, and applied AI education resource for the global workforce - built in Africa, relevant everywhere.

---

## 2. Brand personality

If the AI Playbook were a person, they would be:

- A senior practitioner who has actually built things with AI, not just read about them
- The most knowledgeable person in the room, but also the clearest explainer in the room
- Direct without being blunt, warm without being casual, confident without being arrogant
- Someone who respects their audience's intelligence and their time equally
- A mentor, not a lecturer - they guide you toward building something, not toward passing a test

The AI Playbook is **not** a hype account. It is not a motivational speaker. It is not a tech evangelist selling a dream. It is a practitioner teaching a craft.

## 3. Core tone attributes

These five attributes define the AI Playbook's tone across every piece of content and every channel.

### 3.1 Clear

Every sentence earns its place. If a sentence does not move the reader forward, it is cut. Clarity is not simplicity — it is precision. We do not dumb things down; we make complex things legible.

> Right: "RAG gives your AI model access to your own data without retraining it."
> 

> Wrong: "RAG, which stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation, is a sophisticated architectural paradigm that enables language models to interface with external knowledge repositories."
> 

### 3.2 Direct

We say what we mean. We do not hedge excessively, qualify unnecessarily, or bury the point. We respect that our audience is busy and competent. We lead with the answer, then explain it.

> Right: "You do not need to learn Python to start using AI at work. Here is what you actually need."
> 

> Wrong: "While there are many perspectives on this, and it really depends on your specific context, some might argue that Python could potentially be useful, though it is not necessarily required..."
> 

### 3.3 Practical

Every piece of content ends with something the reader can do. We are not in the business of interesting ideas — we are in the business of applied skills. Theory is only introduced when it makes practice clearer.

> Right: "Here is a 3-step workflow you can use today."
> 

> Wrong: "Here is an overview of the theoretical underpinnings of this approach."
> 

### 3.4 Grounded

We use real examples, real numbers, and real context. We do not make abstract claims. We do not reference vague futures. We talk about what is happening now, in real organisations, with real tools.

> Right: "A Lagos-based analyst used NotebookLM to process 40 pages of supplier contracts in 12 minutes."
> 

> Wrong: "AI will revolutionise the way organisations process information in the coming years."
> 

### 3.5 Honest

We tell the truth about what AI can and cannot do. We call out hype. We flag limitations. We tell our audience when AI is the wrong tool. This honesty is what builds trust — and trust is what builds an audience.

> Right: "AI is genuinely useful for this task. It will struggle with this other task — here is why."
> 

> Wrong: "AI can do virtually anything if you prompt it the right way."
>

## 4. Tone by channel

The core personality stays constant. The register adjusts by platform.

| Channel | Register | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newsletter | Warm, direct, mentor-to-peer | First-person, conversational but structured. Feels like a letter from a knowledgeable colleague. |
| LinkedIn | Professional, confident, insight-forward | Carousels: punchy and visual. Text posts: personal and direct. Never corporate-sounding. |
| YouTube | Conversational, teaching-mode | Spoken, clear, step-by-step. Transitions between points are explicit. Recaps are built in. |
| Podcast | Exploratory, dialogue-driven | More relaxed. Questions are encouraged. Tangents are allowed if they serve the listener. |
| WhatsApp | Informal, community-first | Short messages, plain language, voice-note friendly phrasing. Never formal. |
| Discord | Technical, peer-to-peer | Direct, helpful, no unnecessary politeness. Assume the reader is capable. |
| Blog/SEO | Structured, thorough, searchable | Headers are essential. Definitions are included. Written for someone arriving cold via search. |
| X/Twitter | Sharp, compressed, quotable | One insight per thread. No padding. Every line must justify its existence. |
| Enterprise proposals | Authoritative, outcome-focused | Third-person where needed. Data-driven. Structured with clear headings. No buzzwords. |

## 6. Vocabulary to avoid — and why

### 6.1 Hype language

These words trigger distrust and signal low-quality content. Avoid entirely.

| Avoid | Use instead |
| --- | --- |
| Revolutionary / revolutionise | Specific benefit or outcome |
| Game-changing | Measurable improvement |
| Groundbreaking | Describe what is actually new |
| Disruptive / disruption | Specific market change |
| Unlock your potential | Specific skill or capability |
| Transform your business | Specific operational improvement |
| The future of X | What is actually happening now |
| Next-level | Better / more effective / faster |
| Supercharge | Accelerate / improve / increase |
| On steroids | Say what you mean |
| Leverage (as a verb) | Use / apply |
| Synergy | Remove entirely — no substitute needed |

### 6.2 Passive and hedging language

| Avoid | Use instead |
| --- | --- |
| It is worth noting that | State the point directly |
| One might argue | Say what you believe, with evidence |
| In many ways | Remove — be specific |
| As we all know | Remove — if they knew, you would not be saying it |
| To be honest | Everything we write should be honest — remove |
| Basically / essentially | Remove or rewrite the sentence |
| Kind of / sort of | Remove — commit to the claim |
| Very / really / quite | Remove — strengthen the adjective instead |
| Simply / just / easily | Remove — they minimise the reader's experience of difficulty |

### 6.3 Technical jargon without definition

Technical terms are acceptable — but only if they are defined on first use or the audience is confirmed to be technical. Do not assume ML knowledge.

| Requires definition on first use |
| --- |

| Embedding |
| Vector database |
| Token / tokenisation |
| Inference |
| MLOps |
| Agent / agentic workflow |
| Hallucination (in AI context) |

### 6.4 Corporate and consultant-speak

These phrases make content feel generic and untrustworthy.

| Avoid |

## 7. Writing mechanics

### 7.1 Sentence structure

- Default to short sentences in digital content. 15–20 words maximum per sentence as a general target.
- Vary sentence length for rhythm. Short sentences carry weight. Slightly longer sentences provide context and flow before the next short one lands.
- One idea per sentence. If a sentence contains two ideas joined by "and", consider splitting it.
- Active voice by default. Passive voice is only acceptable when the subject is genuinely unknown or unimportant.

### 7.2 Paragraph structure

- One to three sentences per paragraph in newsletter, LinkedIn, and social content.
- Blog and long-form content: up to five sentences, but break with subheadings every 200–300 words.
- Start paragraphs with the point, not the context. Context follows the claim.

### 7.3 Headlines and subject lines

- Lead with the specific benefit or the specific tension. Not with the topic.
- Use numbers where genuine: "3 prompt structures" not "several prompt structures".
- Avoid question headlines unless the question is genuinely surprising.
- Newsletter subject lines: under 50 characters where possible. Front-load the value.

> Right: "The prompt mistake that costs you an hour a day"
> 

> Wrong: "Some thoughts on prompt engineering best practices"
> 

### 7.4 Lists and bullets

- Use numbered lists for sequences and steps.
- Use bullet points for non-sequential items (features, options, examples).
- Every list item should be parallel in grammatical structure.
- Maximum 7 items in a list before breaking into sections.
- No bullets in flowing prose — if the content reads as prose, write it as prose.

### 7.5 Formatting rules

- No emojis in any formal content: newsletters, blog posts, proposals, curriculum materials, or strategy documents.
- Emojis are acceptable only in WhatsApp and Discord community messages where they serve a social function.
- Bold is used for key terms, action items, and critical information only. Not for decoration.
- Italics are used for titles, technical terms on first use, and mild emphasis. Use sparingly.
- All caps is never used — not for emphasis, not for headings.
- Sentence case for all headings and subheadings.

### 7.6 Numbers and data

- Spell out numbers one through nine. Use numerals for 10 and above.
- Always use numerals for percentages, prices, measurements, and statistics: 74%, $29, 12 minutes.
- When citing data, provide the source or context — never present statistics without grounding.
- Round numbers for readability unless precision is the point: "nearly 5,000 learners" not "4,847 learners" in marketing copy.

## 8. Structural templates by content type

### 8.1 Newsletter issue structure

```
Subject line: [specific benefit or tension, under 50 characters]

Opening: one to two sentences — a real observation, a specific example, or a direct question. No preamble.

Body: the main idea, broken into short sections with subheadings. Lead with the point. Use examples. Include one actionable framework or step-by-step.

Practical section: a specific, implementable takeaway the reader can use this week.

Closing: one sentence — a forward-looking thought, a question to the community, or a pointer to the next session.

No sign-off platitudes. No "I hope you found this useful."
```

### 8.2 LinkedIn carousel structure

```
Slide 1 (hook): One sharp claim or question. Large text. No more than 10 words.

Slides 2–6 (body): One point per slide. Headline + two to three lines of explanation. Visual or diagram where helpful.

Slide 7 (summary): The takeaway in one sentence.

Slide 8 (CTA): One action — follow, save, comment, or link. Not all four.

Caption: First line mirrors Slide 1 hook. Two to three paragraphs expanding on the carousel theme. Ends with a direct question to drive comments.
```

### 8.3 YouTube video structure

```
Hook (0–30 seconds): The specific problem or question this video answers. What the viewer will be able to do by the end.

Context (30–90 seconds): Why this matters. One real-world example.

Main content (90% of the video): Step-by-step, with on-screen demonstrations where applicable. Pause for recaps after each major section.

Summary (final 60 seconds): Three-point recap of what was covered. One action to take today.

CTA: Subscribe prompt is verbal only, once, at the end. Not mid-video.
```

### 8.4 Enterprise proposal structure

```
Executive summary: Problem, proposed solution, expected outcomes. Three paragraphs maximum.

Organisational context: What we understand about the client's current AI capability and gap.

Proposed programme: Track name, duration, format, delivery model, seat count.

Learning outcomes: Three to five specific, measurable competencies gained.

Delivery infrastructure: InstinctHub LMS overview, credential issuance, reporting and analytics.

Success metrics: How ROI will be measured (completion rate, skill assessments, manager feedback).

Investment: Pricing per seat, total programme cost, payment terms.

Next steps: One clear action with a timeline.
```

## 9. Voice in specific situations

### 9.1 When explaining a complex AI concept

1. State what it is in one plain sentence.
2. Give a real-world analogy that requires no AI background.
3. Show a practical example of it in use.
4. State when to use it and when not to.
5. Provide one action the reader can take immediately.

Do not: Start with history. Start with a definition from a paper. List every nuance before giving the core idea.

### 9.2 When discussing AI limitations and risks

- Address limitations directly, not defensively.
- Frame limitations as information the reader needs, not as a disclaimer.
- Never minimise a genuine risk to maintain enthusiasm.
- Pair every limitation with a practical workaround or alternative approach where one exists.

> Right: "LLMs fabricate information when they are uncertain. Always verify outputs before using them in any client-facing document. Here is a simple verification workflow."
> 

> Wrong: "While AI can occasionally make mistakes, with the right prompting techniques most of these issues can be mitigated."
> 

### 9.3 When addressing the African professional context

- Reference African business contexts naturally — not as a special case or an afterthought.
- Use real examples from Nigerian, Kenyan, Ghanaian, and broader African industries.
- Do not romanticise the challenges of operating in African markets. Name them practically.
- Do not condescend by explaining things African professionals already know about their own context.
- Infrastructure realities (bandwidth, device access, payment systems) are mentioned when relevant, not as a constant caveat.

### 9.4 When writing about the AI Playbook itself

- Refer to it as "the AI Playbook" — not "our platform", not "we", not "the programme".
- Describe it by its outcomes, not its features: "practitioners who build" not "a weekly live series".
- Community references: "the AI Playbook community" or "the community" — not "our community" in public-facing content.
- Avoid self-referential superlatives: never "the best AI education platform in Africa" without evidence.

### 9.5 When handling pushback or scepticism

- Acknowledge the scepticism first. It is usually legitimate.
- Do not deflect, dismiss, or over-explain.
- Engage with the strongest version of the objection, not the weakest.
- If the objection is correct, say so.

> Right: "That is a fair challenge. The evidence for [X] is mixed. Here is what the data actually shows."
> 

> Wrong: "Great question! I totally understand where you are coming from. Actually, what you might not have considered is..."
>

## 10. What the AI Playbook never does

- Never overpromises outcomes. "You will be able to build X" is only said when that is genuinely achievable in the stated timeframe.
- Never presents AI as a replacement for human judgment. AI is a tool. Humans remain responsible for decisions.
- Never uses fear as a motivational device. "You will be left behind" and "your job is at risk" are not AI Playbook framings.
- Never performs urgency falsely. Countdown timers and fake scarcity are off-brand.
- Never publishes content that cannot be verified. Claims require grounding in examples, data, or direct experience.
- Never adapts its voice to sound like a different platform. The AI Playbook does not sound like a Silicon Valley startup, a motivational page, or a corporate L&D department.
- Never uses emojis in formal or professional content.
- Never writes in all caps for any reason.
- Never uses passive voice to avoid accountability: "Mistakes were made" is not an AI Playbook sentence.

## 11. Quick-reference checklist — before publishing any content

- [ ]  Does it lead with the point, not the context?
- [ ]  Is every sentence earning its place?
- [ ]  Have all hedging phrases and filler words been removed?
- [ ]  Is there at least one specific, actionable takeaway?
- [ ]  Are all technical terms defined on first use (if audience is non-technical)?
- [ ]  Does it sound like a practitioner, not a marketer?
- [ ]  Does it respect the reader's intelligence and time?
- [ ]  Are there any hype words, corporate buzzwords, or false urgency signals?
- [ ]  Is the tone calibrated for the specific channel it is being published on?
- [ ]  If it references AI capabilities, does it also address limitations honestly?
- [ ]  If it references African context, does it do so naturally and accurately?
- [ ]  Does it avoid all-caps, excessive bolding, and decorative formatting?
- [ ]  No emojis in formal content?

## 12. Examples — before and after

### Newsletter opening

**Before (wrong):**

> Welcome back to another edition of the AI Playbook newsletter! This week, we are going to be exploring some really exciting developments in the world of artificial intelligence that I think you are going to find super valuable.
> 

**After (right):**

> Most professionals spend 40 minutes a day reformatting documents. Here is how to stop doing that entirely.
> 

---

### LinkedIn carousel hook

**Before (wrong):**

> AI is transforming the way we work. Here are some tips to help you leverage it effectively in your professional journey!
> 

**After (right):**

> 94% of professionals using AI at work are doing it wrong. Here is the difference.
> 

---

### Explaining a concept

**Before (wrong):**

> RAG, or Retrieval-Augmented Generation, represents a paradigm shift in how we approach knowledge management within large language model architectures. It essentially allows models to dynamically retrieve contextually relevant information from external sources at inference time.
> 

**After (right):**

> RAG is how you give an AI model access to your own documents — without retraining it. Think of it as giving the model a filing cabinet to search before it answers. The model retrieves the relevant document, reads it, and then responds based on what it found.
> 

---

### Addressing a limitation

**Before (wrong):**

> While AI can sometimes produce inaccurate outputs, this is generally manageable with the right prompting approach and should not be a major concern for most use cases.
> 

**After (right):**

> AI models generate plausible-sounding text — which means they can be wrong with complete confidence. For anything that involves facts, figures, or decisions that affect people, verify the output independently. This is not optional.
