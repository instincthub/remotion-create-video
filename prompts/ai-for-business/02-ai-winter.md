## VIDEO SCRIPT

What if I told you the most powerful technology on the planet was rejected twice? Not by competitors, but by everyone. Investors, governments, scientists; they all walked away. They called it dead. This is the story of Artificial Intelligence's darkest days, the Artificial Intelligence Winters and why understanding them might be the most important tech history lesson you'll ever learn.

Let's go back to the beginning. The year is 1950. Alan Turing, yes, the codebreaker, publishes a paper asking a wild question: "Can machines think?" He didn't just ask it. He designed a test for it. The Turing Test. That one paper lit a fuse.

Six years later, in 1956, a group of brilliant minds gathered at Dartmouth College. John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, and others came together and coined the term "Artificial Intelligence." Sadly, Turing himself never saw it; he died in 1954. And another computing giant, John von Neumann, was too ill to attend. But that workshop planted the seed for decades of research.

Now here's where it gets interesting.

What even is artificial intelligence?

John McCarthy called it "the science of making intelligent machines." Marvin Minsky said it's about making machines do things that would require intelligence if done by humans. Simple enough, right? But there's a hilarious catch. Every time Artificial Intelligence actually succeeds at something, people stop calling it Artificial Intelligence. "Oh, that's just a calculator. That's just a search engine." There's even a name for this, the Artificial Intelligence Effect. As Larry Tesler put it: "Intelligence is whatever machines haven't done yet." We basically keep moving the goalposts!

So Artificial Intelligence research was rolling along, and then, boom. The first Artificial Intelligence Winter hits. It's the mid 1970s. A British scientist named James Lighthill published a devastating report saying Artificial Intelligence research has failed to deliver on its promises. Governments listened. Funding was slashed across the UK and worldwide. For six years, from 1974 to 1980, Artificial Intelligence was basically left out in the cold.

But then, a comeback! By the mid-1980s, investment banks discovered that neural networks could predict stock prices. Wall Street went wild. Banks were racing to build the smartest trading algorithms. Money was flowing, confidence was soaring, and everyone trusted the machines. What could go wrong? Well… everything. Here's the thing about neural networks: they learn from historical data. They're terrible at handling something they've never seen before. And the stock boom they helped create? That was unprecedented. So when the algorithms all said "Sell!" at the same time, Black Monday happened. The 1987 global financial crash. Artificial Intelligence didn't cause it alone, but it poured gasoline on the fire.

And just like that, the Second Artificial Intelligence Winter arrived. From 1987 to 1993, funding collapsed again. Expert systems fell out of favour. Specialised Artificial Intelligence hardware companies went under. The cycle of hype, disappointment, and abandonment repeated itself.

So what's the lesson here? Every time Artificial Intelligence was overhyped and underdelivered, trust evaporated. But here's the thing: Artificial Intelligence never actually died. It kept evolving quietly in labs and research papers. The breakthroughs we see today in ChatGPT, self-driving cars, and medical Artificial Intelligence are built on decades of work that survived those winters.

The real takeaway? Don't believe the hype. Don't believe the doom. Believe the work. Because the best technology doesn't win by being loud: it wins by being persistent.
