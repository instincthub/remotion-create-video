## VIDEO SCRIPT

**Title:** When Machines Tried to Think Like Experts
**Slug:** expert-systems-rise-and-fall
**Excerpt:** Before modern Artificial Intelligence learned from data, it tried to copy human experts using rules. Here is how expert systems like MYCIN, DENDRAL, and AARON shaped the future and why they struggled to scale.


What if we could copy the mind of a doctor, a chemist, or even an artist… and put it inside a machine?

That was the big dream of the 1970s and 80s.

Before machine learning took over, Artificial Intelligence had a different strategy. Instead of learning from massive datasets, it tried to manually encode human expertise into software. These systems were called Expert Systems.

The idea was simple. If experts make decisions using rules, then let us write down those rules and give them to a computer.

One of the most famous examples was MYCIN. Built at Stanford, it was designed to help doctors diagnose bacterial infections. It used about 350 rules based on the knowledge of infectious disease specialists. It could even explain its reasoning. If you asked why it recommended a treatment, it would walk you through its logic.

In tests, it performed competitively with human experts.

But it was never used in real hospitals.

Why?

Because building and maintaining it was extremely difficult. Medicine evolves. New bacteria emerge. Treatments change. Every update required human experts to manually rewrite rules.

Another example was DENDRAL. It helped chemists determine molecular structures using mass spectrometry data. It even rediscovered scientific rules that were already known and found new ones. Impressive.

But again, it depended on carefully handcrafted expert knowledge.

Then there was AARON, a robotic artist. It could create physical paintings of people and plants. It chose its subjects and composition autonomously. But the style and artistic logic were still programmed by humans.

Here is the core problem: Expert Systems did not learn. They depended on humans to teach them everything in advance.

Imagine trying to write a rule for every possible medical situation. Or every possible chemical structure. Or every possible artistic variation. The number of rules would explode.

This is called the knowledge engineering bottleneck.

It was slow. It was expensive. And it did not scale.

As knowledge changed, the systems had to be constantly updated. If you wanted to move the system to a new domain, you had to start almost from scratch.

Eventually, the field shifted. Instead of telling machines what the rules are, researchers began letting machines discover patterns from data.

That shift changed everything.

Interestingly, some modern technologies feel like a new version of old dreams. For example, today’s deep fake images echo what AARON attempted decades ago. But instead of being hand-programmed, modern systems learn from massive datasets.

So what is the lesson?

Expert Systems were not failures. They were stepping stones. They showed that machines could reason. But they also proved that manually encoding human knowledge does not scale in a complex, changing world.

If you want to understand modern Artificial Intelligence, you have to understand where it started.

Because sometimes, the future is just a smarter version of the past.
